# **My World War II Service in the Army**

I was inducted into the Army the 7th of October 1943 in Los Angeles, CA. I reported to Fort MacArthur on the 23rd of October 1943\. We were given a quick rundown of Army routine and issued our uniforms, given short haircuts, and several shots were administered. Some recruits passed out when they saw the needle. Of course, there were stories circulated about big square needles. I can't recall what shots they were, but our arms were quite swollen for several days. They gave us a preview of getting up quickly in the early morning, by blowing a bugle. You were to be outside in formation in 10 minutes. Your name was called and you answered **Present And Accounted For**. The Sergeant asked those in line who would be interested in **Police Work** to take one step forward. Quite a few thinking that would be a good thing to be in. The Sergeant then said all others line up for breakfast and the Police volunteers would Police the area. After they finished they could go to breakfast. It was a lesson not to volunteer if you didn't know what you were volunteering for.  
After about a week, we were loaded on a train to go to **Basic Training**. None of us recruits had any idea where we were headed. We went north for a while, then we went over the Sierra Nevada Mts. It was a beautiful ride. After a two-day ride we disembarked at **Abilene, Texas**. We were taken by trucks to a camp called, **Camp Barkeley**. As we arrived we looked at our nest homes. They were **Tar Paper Barracks**. The floors were planks and had cracks between the boards. We were told that these barracks were to be clean at all times. Not knowing Texas weather, it didn't sound too hard to do. They showed us how the Army wanted us to keep our **footlockers**. Footlockers are where you keep your underwear, your socks, shaving gear, and all your personal effects. Your shoes were to shine like a new penny. The barracks were to be kept so clean that if the Captain inspected and in wiping with white gloves got them dirty, the whole Platoon would be restricted and no weekend passes would be issued. No one wanted to be guilty of restricting all. There was a lot of soap and water used for **Q'ing** (cleaning) the barracks. After cleaning, then you hoped that the wind wouldn't blow, as the sand would blow up through the cracks in the floor. The inspection would happen after breakfast. They have a saying in Texas, "if you don't like the weather wait 5 minutes and it will change," this was so true.  
**Basic Training** lasted for 12 weeks. We had quite a lot of class work \- learning map reading, compass training, Army etiquette (Yes Sir \- No Sir), proper way to salute a superior, basic first aid, etc. We were assigned specific chores, working **KP** (Kitchen Patrol), **Guard Duty**. Also, we did daily marches with **Galoshes**. We made marches with full backpacks. We carried half a tent in our backpack and at night you pitched tent with another fellow, and you both have your tent halves. We also did night problems where you would be in a **trench** and you had to come out of the trench and creep and **crawl** across a field and they would fire live ammunition over the top of you. You learned soon how to keep down. It was a rainy night when we did this; the trench had about a foot of water in it, so it was real messy to creep and crawl. There was a young fellow who said **He Couldn't Do This**. He was told **He Had To Do It** so "get with it." Well, no sooner than he got out of the trench, he had an **Epileptic Fit**. They had to stop the guns and get a **litter** and carried him off the range. We never saw him again, so I guess he received a **Medical Discharge**.  
We had to take an **Equivalency Test** so they could reassign you to **Officers Training** or **Medical Training at an Army Hospital**. I scored 115 points and was given a choice of doing either training. I was getting a little Army smart and chose the medical training. While in basic training I took up **smoking** as each hour they would take a five-minute break and would say, **"All light up that wants to."** I thought why not join with everyone as they all seemed to enjoy the break. This bad habit lasted about 45 years and became so very hard to stop.  
Finally the day arrived and I was called to go to **Springfield, MO, to O'Reilly General Hospital**. We were assigned to nice white barracks there, so much nicer than the Tar Paper ones in Camp Barkeley. We were told that we would go to classes and also work in the **Wards** at the hospital. I did good in all the classes except **Ceph in Materia Medica**. This involved the use of **mathematics**, where you also had to know about **grams**, and also problems that needed a **Chemistry background**. I never took Chemistry in High school. I was exceptionally good at **bandaging**, and after the class graduated and was to be assigned as a **Unit**, I and another fellow didn't get assigned, as they wanted us to stay a little longer and I was to **teach field bandaging**. I can't remember what Paul Wiehe was to teach.  
I stayed at O'Reilly another month and a half. Then I was sent back to Barkeley for reassignment. I was then reunited with a fellow from San Diego, his name was Sweeney. He had been a **Pharmaceutical Salesman** in civilian life. In Basic Training he had suffered a **groin rupture** and was just recovering as I returned to Barkeley. I was soon on another train headed West. I was on my way to **Camp Beale in California**. It was located near Marysville, CA. This was what they called a **POC** which meant you were going overseas. After a short time there, assignments were being made. They called 99 of us as a unit and instead of going overseas, we were to be sent back to **Camp Gruber in Oklahoma** to form a **Field Evacuation Hospital**. We again had to attend more classes and become a cohesive unit. We became the **135th Evacuation Hospital**. The **Cadre** in our unit was a bunch of **Infantry rejects**; they all had stripes on their uniforms. After all the training some of us had, there was no way to upgrade our ranks and earn more pay. The worst part was, they were to do all the teaching. I fell asleep in a class on **bandaging**, and the Sergeant had a fellow next to me wake me up. He said, "I suppose you know all about this subject." I said **"Yes Sir, and could do that without a bandage, blindfolded."** Without what, he had me come up front, blindfold me, and said **"Go ahead."** I did the bandage, and after class I ran to report him to the **Captain's office**. The First Sergeant wasn't going to let me talk to the Captain, as he was a friend of the fellow teaching the class. Well, in the meantime the class instructor came in, and there was some loud exclamation made. The Captain came to the door to see what was happening. He told the First Sergeant to cool down, and he asked me to come into his office. I explained what happened and I told him I would like a transfer to another unit. He said he couldn't do that but I could transfer to the **Motor Pool** if I wanted, and I wouldn't have to attend any more classes. I readily agreed and I really liked Sergeant Garcia who ran the Motor Pool. He made me the **Motor Pool Dispatcher**. Soon after we were on the train and we found our destination to be **Fort Dix near New Brunswick, New Jersey**. After a couple of days there we were taken to New York and boarded a ship headed for **Europe**. We didn't know it but we were in a **99-boat Convoy** headed for **France**, finally going to the **War Zone**. We departed New York the 23rd of March. It was interesting looking either direction to see boats, all in a row, as far as you could see. Each day we practiced drills, in case there would be an emergency. We were to come to the deck for instructions. The ship was three decks deep in the vessel. Many people on the boat were **sea sick**. It was estimated by sick bay about **60% of personnel were up chucking**.  
On about the 5th night around **10 pm** I was laying on my bunk when there was a grinding sound. The boat kind of **lurched**. I thought like we had struck a **sand bar** and it was moving back and forth trying to **dislodge itself**. Then the **emergency bells and lights** came on and we were to go to the **deck**. Several fellows completely lost it. A big fellow from Oklahoma came running and flailing his arms. We ran over quite a few people and really **messed up the evacuation drill**. I and a fellow named Dean decided there was no way to try to get out, and that we would just wait and go out when things quieted down. What happened was not a **torpedo**, but the night was quite **foggy**, and the boat to our right mistook a **blinker signal** and turned, and came right in **front of us**. We hit them right in the middle of their boat. **It hit them right in the Crew Quarters**. We heard it **killed seven of their crew**. The scraping sound we heard was our boat trying to dislodge from them. Their cargo was **Hi-Octane Gasoline**. If we had hit them 10 ft either way we all would have been blown out of the water. The next morning the Captain said we did so good in the **emergency drill** that we wouldn't have to practice anymore. I figured the drill was a **complete flop**, and that we were so fortunate to be alive. Our ship was to go to **Southampton, England**, for repairs. We arrived in Southampton **April 6** and was transferred to an **English (Luny Boat)** to go across the **English Channel** to **France**. We landed on the coast near **Normandy Beach**. We were assigned to **Camp Lucky Strike**, a **Tent Camp**, near **Rouen, France**. We never got our tents put up then we were told to **pack up**, and that we were now in the **Second Army** and that we were going to **catch up to Patton's Army** on his drive through France and into **Germany**. We went through the **Maginot Line** and into **Germany** through **Aachen**. The **City of Aachen** was a completely **bombed out place**. I don't believe that a single building downtown had four outside walls standing. We hurried through as we were trying to catch **Patton**. We passed a number of **villages** with no sign of warfare. Finally we reached **Cologne**, only to see it **completely devastated**. At this point we received orders to go to a town called **Hambourne** and set up our **Hospital** there. We arrived there and **took over their hospital** and all of the houses adjacent to the hospital to house our troops. The **Motor Pool** took over a large garage and had a lot of room for our vehicles. We all learned a few words in **German**. We would **exchange food and candy bars for schnapps**. **Schnapps is a very fine white wine**. We also were able to trade for things like **cameras, binoculars, etc.**  
While in **Hambourne**, the **Supply Lieutenant, Lt. Intrater** was going to make a trip to several **Supply Depots**. Being I was the **Motor Pool Dispatcher**, I chose to drive him. A Sergeant from the Supply room was to go along. Our first stop was in **Paris**. We visited a **wine cellar** under the street. The wine bottles were stored on shelves that could be rotated. It took **years** for the wine to complete the circle in the **aging process**. After touring the wine cellar we came up into a large foyer, and they had us **sample the wine**. It was a white or clear as water wine and was very good. After leaving **Paris** we went South to **Lyon, St**. It was a beautiful city. We went to a **supply depot** several miles out in the country. **Lt. Intrater** got both a **Sergeant Golden and myself an Eisenhower jacket**. To make a long story short, we had a lot of fun and the trip took **11 days** to go where the Lt. had business.  
When we got back to **Hambourne**, the **War in Europe was coming to a close**. Shortly after we were ordered to go to a **Camp near Marseille**. It was called an **R & R Camp**, and was in the **desert**. **R & R** really means **Rest & Recuperation**. I really think they wanted us close to a port so we could be sent to the **Pacific Area**. After several weeks we received orders to load on a ship going to the **Orient** for **occupation duty**. An **Armistice** brought the war in **Europe to the end**. Now the problem was to bring our **servicemen home to be discharged**. A **point system** was devised to send the men who had been in the **European area the longest to be first to go home**. It so happened that we had been first loaded in our boat, then it was decided to load the **highest point men** on our boat and to send this boat to the **U.S.A**. They decided to leave us as they didn't want to unload our equipment. We left **France on the 17th of August** and for some reason after leaving the **Mediterranean** we went **north** to cross the **Atlantic by the North Atlantic route**. We ran into a **big storm** and I just want to say I never believed waves could be so large. I'm sure that **waves were 50 or more feet in height**. We were **tossed about like a leaf by those huge waves**. We were told to stay below but, I just had to have a look. One look is all I wanted so I never forget that ocean. We arrived in **New York on Aug. 27, 1945**. Again we were sent to **Fort Dix in New Jersey**. We spent some time there and finally put on a train to go to **California**. Due to **heavy rail traffic** we spent **7 days** crossing good old **U.S.A**. We arrived at **Fort MacArthur Feb. 28, 1946**. After a period of time I was **discharged on March 31, 1946**. My **total service time was 2 years, 3 mos, and 28 days**. I received **4 medals** and a lot of **valuable experience as a soldier**.
